*Prestazione occasionale*, Italy's occasional self-employed work, isn't reserved for Italians. Foreigners can use it too, whether they live in Italy or abroad, and the rules are the same as for anyone else. What changes the tax treatment is not your passport but your **tax residence**. This guide covers residency, the documents you need, the two most common cross-border situations, social security and a practical checklist.

## Tax residence, not nationality

Italy taxes people differently depending on where they are tax resident:

- **Italian tax residents** are taxed in Italy on their worldwide income. Occasional work for any client, Italian or foreign, goes in the Italian tax return.
- **Non-residents** are taxed in Italy only on Italian-source income. For occasional work, that means income from activities carried out in Italy (art. 23 TUIR).

So a foreigner who lives in Italy is usually treated exactly like an Italian, while an Italian citizen who has moved abroad may be a non-resident.

## Are you an Italian tax resident?

Since 2024 (art. 2 TUIR, as amended by D.Lgs. 209/2023), you are an Italian tax resident if, for most of the tax year, you have at least one of these in Italy:

- your **residence**, meaning the place where you habitually live;
- your **domicile**, meaning the place where your personal and family relationships are mainly centred;
- your **physical presence**.

"Most of the year" means at least 183 days, or 184 in a leap year, and fractions of a day count. In 2026, which is not a leap year, the figure is 183 days. Being registered in the Italian population register (*anagrafe*) for most of the year creates a presumption that you are resident, but you can prove otherwise.

If another country also considers you resident, the double tax treaty between Italy and that country, if there is one, decides which country has priority through its tie-breaker rules.

<Callout type="tip" title="Moving during the year">
Italian law doesn't split the tax year. If the residency tests are met for most of the year, you are resident for the whole year, and only a few tax treaties allow the year to be split. If you move in or out of Italy mid-year, check your position with a commercialista.
</Callout>

## Codice fiscale and permits

To issue a receipt and be paid by an Italian client, you need an Italian *codice fiscale* (tax code). You can request it from an Agenzia delle Entrate office in Italy or, if you live abroad, through an Italian consulate.

EU citizens can work in Italy without a work permit. If you are a non-EU citizen living in Italy, you need a residence permit (*permesso di soggiorno*) that allows you to work: not every permit does, and conditions vary, so check with the Questura, a CAF or a *patronato* before you start. If you're coming to Italy just for the job, check the entry and work rules for your nationality before you travel.

## Italian residents with foreign clients

If you live in Italy and work occasionally for a company or person abroad:

- **No Italian withholding.** A foreign client with no Italian presence isn't an Italian withholding agent, so you receive the gross amount. The receipt states why: "Compenso non soggetto a ritenuta d'acconto: committente non residente privo di stabile organizzazione in Italia" (no withholding tax: non-resident client without a permanent establishment in Italy).
- **Declare the income in Italy**, in your 730 or Redditi PF return, as for any occasional income.
- **Foreign tax.** If tax was withheld in the client's country, you may be able to claim a credit for it in Italy. Ask a CAF or commercialista.
- **INPS.** The €5,000 threshold counts all your occasional income, but contributions are collected through Italian withholding agents. If you go over €5,000 mainly with foreign clients, check your position with a CAF or patronato.

A bilingual receipt, in Italian plus the client's language, saves a lot of questions. The [receipt template guide](/en/invoice-template-italy/) shows the wording.

## Non-residents with Italian clients

If you are not an Italian tax resident and an Italian company, entity or professional pays you for work carried out in Italy, the client withholds **30% as a final tax** (art. 25, paragraph 2, DPR 600/1973). "Final" means the withholding settles your Italian tax on that income, unlike the 20% advance withheld from residents.

On a €1,000 job, the client withholds €300 and pays you €700. Private individuals in Italy are not withholding agents, so they don't withhold; if your client is a private individual, ask a CAF or commercialista whether you need to declare that income in Italy.

<Callout type="warning" title="Using a tax treaty">
A double tax treaty between Italy and your country may reduce or remove the 30%. To apply it, the client needs a certificate of tax residence from your country's tax authority and the relevant Agenzia delle Entrate form. Give them to the client before you're paid, or the client will apply the full 30%.
</Callout>

Two more points:

- **Your home country.** You will normally also have to declare the income where you live, under local rules, which may give you a credit for the Italian tax. Check with a local adviser.
- **Work done abroad.** Under art. 23 TUIR, non-residents' occasional income is taxable in Italy only for activities carried out in Italy. If you do the job entirely from abroad, ask the client's accountant how they will treat the payment before you issue the receipt.

## Social security in the EU

Social security follows separate rules from tax. Within the EU, if you live in one country and work in another, or work in several, EU coordination rules generally make you subject to one country's social security legislation at a time, and an A1 certificate can show which country that is.

How this interacts with INPS contributions on occasional work above €5,000 depends on your circumstances. Ask INPS or a patronato in Italy, and the social security institution in your home country, before you assume either way.

## Practical checklist

1. Work out your tax residence for the year: residence, domicile or presence in Italy for at least 183 days, plus any anagrafe registration.
2. Get an Italian codice fiscale.
3. If you are a non-EU citizen in Italy, make sure your permit allows you to work.
4. Check that the job is genuinely occasional; habitual work needs a Partita IVA. See the [guide to working without a VAT number](/en/freelance-italy-without-vat/).
5. Identify your client: an Italian withholding agent, a private individual or a foreign client.
6. If you are non-resident and a treaty applies, give the client your residence certificate and the Agenzia delle Entrate form before payment.
7. Keep track of your occasional income for the calendar year, for [the €5,000 INPS threshold](/en/5000-euro-limit-italy/).
8. Issue a receipt with the correct withholding wording, bilingual if it helps the client.
9. Declare the income where required: in Italy if you're resident, and in your home country if you're not.

## FAQ

### Do I need a Partita IVA as a foreigner?

Not for occasional work. The rules are the same as for Italians: genuinely occasional jobs can be billed with a receipt, while habitual or organised work needs a Partita IVA.

### Does my nationality change the withholding rate?

No, your tax residence does. Italian residents paid by an Italian withholding agent have 20% withheld as an advance; non-residents have 30% withheld as a final tax unless a treaty applies; private and foreign clients don't withhold.

### I moved to Italy in March 2026. Am I resident this year?

Very likely, if you live here from March to December: that's well over 183 days, and Italian law doesn't split the year, so you would be resident for all of 2026. A treaty may change the outcome if your previous country also treats you as resident, so check with a commercialista.

### My foreign client wants an invoice. What do I send?

Your prestazione occasionale receipt, ideally bilingual. Without a Partita IVA you can't issue a fattura, and the receipt is the correct Italian document for occasional work.
